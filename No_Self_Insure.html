No Need to Self-Insure

Two of the most common objections to long-term care insurance — and why the math does not work in your favor when you go it alone.

Common Objection 1

"I can self-fund the risk of long-term care and pay for it myself."

While many people think long-term care is an asset issue, it is really an income issue.

Consider this scenario: A married couple has $2 million in assets to drive their retirement income. If one of them has a $1.5 million long-term care event, their income drops by 75%. While they may have enough assets to cover the risk on paper, the retirement income they once counted on could dwindle much sooner than planned.

Self-funding sounds reasonable until it is not. The financial damage is not just to the person receiving care — it is to the entire household. The healthy spouse is left with a fraction of the income they planned their retirement around, at a time when they are least able to recover it.

Common Objection 2

"Why do I need lifetime benefits? Average care is only 2 to 3 years."

Many people have been misled into thinking they should buy long-term care insurance to protect against the average risk of two to three years — instead of protecting against catastrophic risk.

Think of it this way: The risk of Alzheimer's should be treated the same as the risk of getting cancer. You would not want health insurance that did not cover cancer — and nor should you want long-term care coverage that does not cover the risk of Alzheimer's.

Buying coverage based on the average is like buying fire insurance that only covers small kitchen fires — it misses the point entirely. The average is what happens to most people. The catastrophe is what financially destroys a family.
